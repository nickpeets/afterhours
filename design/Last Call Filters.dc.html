THE RULES
Who controls a filter
The wearer picks, from a set the show curates. No open camera roll, no user-uploaded masks — every option is pre-approved so nothing offensive reaches air, and picking is free choice within a closed list, same shape as a costume rack. The host can force any filter off at any time (a standing moderation power, unused in the normal case); the show forces it off automatically the moment that suitor is asked a question (frame 4). No one else — not the crowd, not another chair — can touch it.
One face, every viewer
No split view, in either direction — the constraint at the top of this page. Whatever the host, the crowd, and every other chair see is the one track that suitor published. That also means a filter can't be a scouting tool: it doesn't let anyone see something others can't.
The reveal is forced, not chosen
It isn't his call, her call, or a clock — it's the spotlight. A filter is armor for the bench and the open floor, where nothing is riding on your face yet. The instant the host ASKs someone, the game needs a real read on a real face, so the mask drops automatically at that beat, same instant the spotlight ring appears. That's the difference between a toy and a mechanic: a toy would let him keep it on while answering; this makes the drop mean something, because it's involuntary and it's public.
He can also drop it early, any time, for free — that direction is always his choice. He just can't keep it once he's asked.
Filters vs. the two other mechanics
The Moment strips it — that's the point of buying it. The amber pairing rim means someone paid for her undivided, unmasked attention. A filter surviving that would make the purchase worthless, so both faces in the pair go bare the instant the moment starts, whether or not either was wearing one going in.
The blind bench composes for free. The host's silhouette rendering already strips every facial detail before a filter would even apply — there's nothing left to mask. The crowd, who do see bench faces, see the filter exactly as they'd see it on a seated chair. No new rule needed; the two mechanics just don't touch.
